# Limitations

Helios is an early-warning **observatory**, not a complete map of AI infrastructure. Users must treat absence of evidence as a coverage gap unless a source proves a negative.

## Access and coverage

1. **ACC eDocket is fixture-only.** Transmission and substation filings are the highest-weight stage signals, but live search is a stateful ASP.NET UI without a documented API. Helios ships a parser tested against recorded dockets; it does **not** scrape the interactive search. Stage 3 recall is therefore incomplete for projects that only appear in ACC filings.
2. **Assessor history is truncated.** The parcel layer exposes the current deed, not the full transfer chain, so land-assembly timelines understate intermediate buyers.
3. **OSM is incomplete.** Missing substations are not evidence that none exist. Transmission geometries from Overpass `out center` are centroids; distances are approximate.
4. **EPA ECHO rate limits.** The public API throttles aggressive clients. Helios self-limits and uses fixtures in CI; a throttled live run is a temporary gap, not proof of no generators.
5. **Mesa planning agendas and dust-control attributes** are not yet automated. Commercial building permits are ingested with address-only matching (no coordinates in source).
6. **No satellite observations.** Copernicus Sentinel-2 is declared in the registry as `planned` with no connector at all. A fixture-backed satellite stub was deliberately removed rather than kept, because shipping one would have implied an imagery capability Helios does not have.
7. **Only one region is actually read, and "read" means sites.** `helios_domain.regions` names nine US regions; exactly one — East Valley, Arizona — is `ACTIVE`. The rest are `DECLARED`: in scope, and holding no sites. A region appearing in the registry is not a claim that Helios is watching it. The two hard blockers to minting a site elsewhere (an `AZ-` project-code prefix and a `Maricopa` county default) are removed, but no connector reads another region's *parcels* yet. One national source is read everywhere (item 8), which is why a state can show reported facilities while its region still reads `declared`. Those are different claims and the UI keeps them in separate columns.
8. **Helios reads facilities nationally and builds sites in one region.** The ECHO connector sweeps every state in one request via the `p_ncs` NAICS filter (`helios ingest epa-echo-air-facilities-national`). ECHO reports 447 matching rows, delivers 447, and 440 are distinct — its own count includes repeated RegistryIDs — of which 410 survive hosting classification, across 39 states. They are recorded as a fixture and published: `/analytics/national-coverage` and `/map/facilities`. **These facilities are not sites, and the two counts must never be added.** A facility is one federal record saying a permitted Clean Air Act facility carries a hosting NAICS code. A site is Helios's own hypothesis that a set of parcels forms one development, and it requires parcel geometry and ownership, which are published county by county. Site building attaches permits by proximity, so a facility links to a site only where parcels have been read: six do, all in Arizona, at 50–427 m. Everywhere else a facility stands alone. A state showing facilities and no sites is unread, not empty, and no figure here is a count of data centres in that state. Parcel coverage is the blocker, and it is per-county: every county publishes its assessor data differently, or not at all. Two refinements, measured against a second county rather than assumed. First, the blocker is narrower than "parcel data": Loudoun County publishes parcel geometry and acreage through the same ArcGIS REST API Helios already speaks, and publishes no ownership table beside it. Helios clusters on adjacency **and** related ownership, never adjacency alone, so ownership is the field that decides whether clustering can run at all — and it is the field most often withheld. Second, the discovery step cannot be automated away: searching open-data portals by keyword returns wrong-jurisdiction layers and private republications that are indistinguishable from authoritative ones at the API surface. Each county's endpoint has to be identified deliberately, for the same reason Helios declines the HIFLD mirrors.
9. **Area totals are published on different clocks.** EIA's retail sales file currently stops at 2020 while its generation capacity file runs to 2024, so two figures sitting side by side describe different years. Each carries its own `reference_year` rather than being aligned, because matching another dataset's staleness is worse than showing the gap.
10. **Resolution mismatch between water and electricity.** USGS gives water withdrawals per county; EIA gives retail electricity sales per state and no public source breaks those sales to county nationally. A statewide denominator is much weaker than a county one for a metro-scale region, and it understates the local share of any figure compared against it. Every stored row carries its own `area_kind`, and the API returns the mismatch as an explicit note rather than papering over it.
11. **The water total is 2015 and there will not be a newer one soon.** USGS publishes county-level water use on a five-yearly cycle, but the 2020 compilation dropped the county breakdown entirely. 2015 is the most recent county figure that exists, not the most recent Helios bothered to fetch. Comparing a present-day site estimate against it is comparing across a decade.
12. **Helios does not know substation capacity and will not estimate it.** OSM carries substation voltage and HIFLD carried the same attribute; neither carries transformer capacity in MW, and FERC Form 715 is access-restricted as CEII. Substation MW capacity is not publicly obtainable at national scale. No substation in the API or the UI carries a capacity or a utilisation number, and none will be invented to fill the gap.
13. **US substation coverage is OpenStreetMap's, and the intended replacement no longer exists.** HIFLD's national substation layer (69 kV and above) was to be the fix for OSM's contributor-dependent completeness. DHS has withdrawn public access: the HIFLD Open catalogue API answers `401 — private org id ... is not accessible`, and nothing federal has replaced it, the EIA Energy Atlas carrying no substation, transmission or power-plant layer at all. Undated copies survive on university and state-agency ArcGIS servers with empty copyright fields and no maintained licence. Helios will not ingest one: substation geometry entering the graph as reported fact by way of an unattributed mirror is a worse position than OSM's honest under-coverage. The source is declared in the registry as `withdrawn` so the gap stays visible rather than silently absent.
14. **The national facility layer counts what has been *mapped*, not what exists.** The observatory reads 1,853 US data centres from OpenStreetMap. Every one is a real record with real coordinates, but the set is assembled by volunteers and its completeness is unknown and uneven. Northern Virginia is mapped in detail because people there map it; a county showing nothing has not been shown to be empty, only to be unmapped. No figure derived from this layer is a census, and the difference cannot be measured, because no authoritative count of US data centres exists to compare against.
15. **OpenStreetMap carries no construction dates, so the growth curve is a mapping curve.** `start_date` coverage across the US data-centre set is **zero**. The only time axis available is when contributors edited the map, and the two are not the same: one Loudoun County building was first drawn in January 2010 and only tagged as a data centre in December 2016. The published series is therefore explicitly *observed* — when OpenStreetMap recorded a facility — and never *reported* construction. It follows that the near-zero readings before 2017 describe the adoption of the `telecom=data_center` tag rather than the state of the country, and the charts hatch and label that stretch instead of cropping it. A removal is subject to the same care: ohsome reports a deletion when an element stops matching the filter, which happens both when a building leaves the map and when a mapper retags it, so the wording everywhere is "removed from OpenStreetMap" and never "demolished".
16. **Per-facility power and water are shares of a national total, and they are upper bounds.** No public source meters an individual data centre. Helios takes LBNL's reported national figures — 192 TWh of electricity for 2024, 17.4 billion gallons of direct water for 2023 — and allocates them across mapped *buildings* by floor area, so the shares re-sum to the published total by construction. The unverifiable assumption is that OpenStreetMap knows about every US data centre. It does not, and every facility missing from the map has its consumption redistributed onto the ones present, so each per-facility figure is an upper bound of unknown looseness. Floor area is also only a proxy for capacity, because dense multi-storey halls are under-weighted against sprawling single-storey ones. That proxy currently gives Virginia 4,972 MW against the roughly 4,100 MW Virginia's own JLARC study attributes to Northern Virginia alone — the same method gave 2,255 MW while campus land parcels were pooled into the weight, which item 19 records. Electricity and water additionally rest on different years, and are carried with separate `reference_year` values rather than aligned.
17. **State generating capacity is not headroom.** Helios publishes what a state can generate, not what is spare. Existing demand already consumes most of it, and how much is not in any source Helios reads. A site's share of total capacity therefore says nothing about whether that site can be connected — that is an interconnection question settled by filings Helios cannot access.
18. **The national grid layer is mapper-recorded, cut at 69 kV, and shows adjacency rather than supply.** The substation and power-plant layers come from OpenStreetMap and inherit its unevenness exactly as the facility layer does — an area with no substation drawn has not been shown to have none. Substations are included only where a `voltage` tag parses to at least 69 kV, so a transmission yard whose voltage a mapper left blank or wrote as free text is absent from the layer entirely, and that absence correlates with how thoroughly a region has been mapped rather than with what is built there. Voltage is reported as the highest level in the tag, which for a transforming substation is a list. Plant capacity is whatever `plant:output:electricity` contains; where that is unparseable the figure is left blank rather than guessed, and a bare number too large to be megawatts is discarded rather than read as one. Most importantly, drawing a data centre beside a substation shows proximity and nothing else: whether any power is contracted from it is settled by interconnection filings Helios cannot read, and in several regions those queues now run for years.
19. **A megawatt figure is given only to facilities mapped as buildings, and that leaves real load unattributed.** The three OpenStreetMap tags Helios selects on are used both for individual machine halls and for the boundary of the land a campus occupies, and once a polygon is reduced to square metres the two are indistinguishable. They are not the same measurement: allocating a national floor-area total by a figure that mixes them sent 82% of LBNL's reported 2024 electricity to geometry that is not a building — 62.7% to 174 land parcels covering 72 km², and 20.0% to 45 sites tagged as under construction, which consumed none of what that total measures. Helios now weights building floor area alone, and the consequence is that a campus mapped only as a parcel contributes nothing to its county's estimate. Racine County, Wisconsin holds Microsoft's Mount Pleasant campus across 4.5 km² of mapped land and two mapped buildings, so its 30 MW is an accounting of those two buildings and not of that campus. The affected regions say so on their own pages. This trades a large, invisible error for a smaller one that is stated, but it is still an error, and it always understates.


## Analytical

1. **Score conflates “is a data centre?” with “how far along?”** Rule weights partially separate these, but a single 0–100 score remains a compromise until a labelled backtest exists.
2. **Operator identity is usually unknown.** Shell-company indicators never become operator attributions.
3. **Proximity ≠ service.** Nearby substations are inferred dependencies, not interconnection facts.
4. **Standing vs event evidence.** Mis-dating standing assessor classifications as old deed events previously zeroed confidence; the model now exempts standing conditions from staleness, but other sources must set the flag correctly.
5. **No calibrated probabilities.** Weights are not fitted; confidence bands are display buckets.
6. **Helios's share of an area total is a ratio of an inference to a measurement.** The denominator is reported and whole-area, covering every existing user. The numerator is inferred from acreage for sites that in most cases are not built, and the electricity comparison layers an assumed load factor on top of an already-assumed power density in order to turn a capacity into an annual energy figure. The ratio gives a sense of scale. It is not a forecast of what the area will consume, and it is weaker than either number that goes into it.

## Product / ops

1. Single-container local stack — no Kafka/Kubernetes (ADR 0002).
2. Admin API refuses all mutations when `HELIOS_ADMIN_API_TOKEN` is unset.
3. The published GitHub Pages site is a point-in-time snapshot exported from a fixture-seeded database, not a live view of public records. The date is stated on every page.
